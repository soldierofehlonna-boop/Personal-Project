He crouches to look at it properly, taking the weight on the good leg, and works the sock down past the ankle bone.

Swollen. Not the hard, immediate balloon of a break -- just the slow puffiness of a joint asked to do something it never agreed to. He presses with two fingers, finds the sore spot, presses either side of it. Bone's fine. He rolls the foot once each way. It complains. It doesn't lock.

Twenty years of warehouse work gave him a narrow, useless expertise in exactly one injury, and this is it. He can walk on it. He will pay for walking on it. Two separate facts, both true, and no version of the morning where he gets to pick only the first one.

He pulls the sock back up and stands.

The light's low and flat, coming across the slope rather than down it -- early, then, and he's facing roughly into it. Downhill the scrub thins toward open ground. Somewhere past that, maybe a mile, maybe three -- distance is a skill and he doesn't have it here -- a single column of smoke stands up out of the trees. Thin. Steady. Not a fire anyone's worried about. Someone's hearth, tended.

The stonework is the other way. Upslope, two hundred yards at a guess, and two hundred yards is nothing. Two hundred yards is the length of a pick line.

It takes him a while.

The ground is all loose stone under scrub, and every third step the ankle rolls a few degrees further than he wants and he has to stop being a man walking and become a man placing his feet, which is slower and more tiring and the kind of thing you can keep up for about twenty minutes before your hips start arguing too. By the time the wall resolves out of the vines he's breathing harder than two hundred yards has any right to make him breathe, and there's a hot line up the outside of his calf that wasn't there at the bottom.

The wall is old. Not ruined-yesterday old -- old enough that the vines are structural now, holding the stone as much as covering it. Chest height where he reaches it, taller further along, and one corner still carries a section of roof: two heavy lintel stones fallen together into a rough peak, and under them a patch of ground the weather hasn't reached in a long time.

He notices the fire ring before he's properly under the lintels, because it's the only thing in the whole landscape arranged in a circle.

Eight stones, set deliberately, the size a man would carry one-handed. Ash inside, and a scatter of black-ended sticks pushed to the edge the way you push them when you're done and not coming back tonight.

He puts the back of his hand near it. Nothing. Cold through.

But the ash is dry and heaped, not slumped and crusted, and the ground under the lintels is dry too, and there's dew on everything outside the stone that isn't under it. So: no rain since. And the sticks at the edge haven't been rolled or scattered by anything. He hasn't the faintest idea what the weather does here, or what animals there are, or how long dew takes to burn off on a Seedmonth morning in a place he learned the name of never.

What he does know is that eight stones don't arrange themselves, and somebody sat here long enough to want them to.

Against the back wall, where the roof still holds, someone has stacked cut wood -- an armful, no more, butts squared off flat. Not gathered deadfall. Cut, and stacked so it stays dry.

You don't stack wood in a place you're leaving.
